I Research question
Mill gives us a philosophical question about why freedom of expression matters. This research moves from that philosophical question to a sociological one: how do people actually understand their legal freedom of expression?
Research question

How do Kazakhstani journalists and activists who came of age during the Nazarbayev era versus the Tokayev era interpret the meaning and boundaries of the legal right to freedom of expression?

II Why this matters

This isn't just asking whether freedom of expression exists in Kazakhstan on paper. It asks how people who actually work in politically sensitive areas — journalism, activism — understand what that right means and where its limits are.

Freedom of expression is not only determined by what the law formally guarantees, but by how people understand and interpret those legal rights in practice. Journalists and political activists are especially relevant because their work often operates close to the boundaries of politically acceptable speech.

By comparing people who came of age under Nazarbayev and under Tokayev, this project asks whether different political environments produce different understandings of what freedom of expression means and where its legal boundaries lie — and, more broadly, whether institutional political change is reflected in how people actually relate to their legal rights, or whether the political environment someone came of age in leaves a stickier mark than the regime currently in power.

III Epistemological positioning
Interpretivist

This question does not ask whether X causes Y. It asks how people themselves make sense of a contested concept — "freedom of expression" — and whether that sense-making differs depending on the political age they came of age in. That's a meaning-making question, aiming to understand from the inside. Interpretivism fits.

IV Literature review

The literature starts with freedom of expression in Kazakhstan as a legal issue. More recent work asks what freedom of expression means as a political value. Legal consciousness brings in the question of how people themselves understand their relationship with law — Kubal connects legal consciousness to political change directly, while Neundorf and Pop-Eleches give reason to think the political context people experience when they come of age has lasting effects.

The gap: we don't yet know how these different political experiences are reflected in how Kazakhstani journalists and activists interpret freedom of expression across periods.

V Analytical framework

Nazarbayev-era cohort · Tokayev-era cohort

Analytical lens

The study uses legal consciousness as its main analytical lens — how people understand, interpret, and relate to law in their everyday and professional lives. Applied to freedom of expression, this means examining two dimensions of how participants understand the right: first, what they believe it protects (criticism of government, political expression); second, where they believe legitimate restrictions begin (national security, for instance). This lens treats freedom of expression not only as a formal legal rule, but as a right whose meaning and boundaries are actively interpreted by people who work directly in journalism and activism.

Clear expectations

The study expects that journalists and activists who came of age in different political environments may attach different meanings and boundaries to the legal right to freedom of expression — differences in what they understand it to protect, and what restrictions they consider legitimate. But it does not assume the two cohorts will necessarily differ, or that any difference found is explained by generation alone. Similarities, unexpected differences, and differences that cut across the two groups rather than between them are all analytically important.

Positionality

As a younger Kazakhstani researcher studying people who came of age during different political periods, my own experiences and assumptions about political change may differ from those of participants from the Nazarbayev era. That can shape which interpretations I notice, how I read participants' descriptions of freedom of expression, and which differences I treat as significant. To guard against this, the study will keep participants' interpretations analytically separate from my own assumptions, and stay open to findings that challenge the expectation that the two generations understand freedom of expression differently.
